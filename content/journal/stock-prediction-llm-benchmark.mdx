Before the stock predictions, this project mattered to me for a more personal reason. One of my teammates was the person who helped me learn JavaScript. That led me to React, TypeScript, and Next.js, and eventually to working as a full-stack developer. I can draw a fairly direct line from her help to the work I do now.

We built this as a team course project in February 2025. The question sounded simple: after a company reports earnings, can a large language model decide whether its stock will keep moving in the same direction when the market opens, reverse, or stay flat? More specifically, would a model that produced a longer chain of thought, the reasoning text before its final answer, make a more profitable decision?

The answer from our benchmark was no. Every one of the eleven models lost money on average.

## The fifteen minutes before the market opens

An earnings announcement can move a stock while the regular market is closed. A company may report just after 4:00 PM, trade through the evening, then move sharply again when regular trading begins at 9:30 AM. Our model had to make its decision at 9:15 AM, after seeing after-hours data through 7:00 PM and pre-market data from 4:00 AM onward, but before seeing what happened at the open.

Netflix's fourth-quarter 2024 report was the example we used in the presentation. Netflix released earnings after the closing bell on January 21, 2025. Its price jumped in after-hours trading and was still up about 14.67% by 9:15 the following morning. The model then had to decide whether that move would continue or reverse.

This was not a prompt containing only an earnings headline. We assembled a report for each event from several kinds of market data:

- Finnhub supplied actual and estimated earnings per share and revenue.
- Databento supplied daily and minute-level OHLCV data, meaning open, high, low, close, and volume, plus bid-and-ask records for trade simulation.
- SPY, IWM, DIA, and QQQ supplied exchange-traded-fund proxies for the recent direction of the S&P 500, Russell 2000, Dow Jones, and Nasdaq 100.
- MongoDB stored the raw records, transformed time series, generated reports, and model outputs.

The scripts narrowed this data into a chronological description: recent market and stock trends, trading during the earnings day, the after-hours reaction, and pre-market prices through 9:15 AM. The target company's post-open prices were held back for evaluation.

## Turning market data into a trade prompt

The full workflow had five stages. We downloaded earnings and price records, standardized symbols and time series, generated a textual briefing, sent that briefing to each model, then replayed the resulting order against historical one-minute bid-and-ask data. A final analysis stage compared profits, returns, position sizes, exit types, and predicted directions.

</Figure>

Each model received the same role and constraints. It was a short-term trader with `$50,000` in capital and four-times leverage. At 9:30 AM it could buy, sell short, or decline to trade. If it traded, it had to specify the dollar size of the position, a limit price for taking profit, and a stop price for limiting a loss. The prompt asked it to risk at most one percent of its capital and close any remaining position by 4:00 PM.

We also included three completed examples, one long trade, one short trade, and one decision not to trade. The final line had to follow a parseable form such as:

```text
BUY +$27,400 NFLX; SELL LMT @90.00 STP -1%
```

That output format turned prose into something the evaluation script could execute. The parser checked the symbol, position sign, limit, and stop. It entered long positions at the available ask or short positions at the bid. It then stepped through one-minute best-bid-and-offer records until the target or stop condition appeared. A position still open at the end of the session was closed at the market close.

This part changed the question from “does the answer sound financially informed?” to “what would this exact order have returned?” A convincing explanation could still produce an invalid stop, an impossible limit, or a large loss.

The shared implementation is available in [our GitHub repository](https://github.com/b1f6c1c4/earnings-llm). It is a sequence of fourteen Node.js scripts followed by a Jupyter notebook for the analysis. The downloaded market dataset and generated analysis files are not included, so the repository documents the implementation rather than providing a self-contained reproduction.

## Comparing eleven models

We tested eleven model configurations across up to 120 earnings events:

- DeepSeek R1 at 671B, 70B, and 7B
- Gemini 2.0 Thinking, Gemini 2.0 Flash, and Gemini 1.5 Flash
- Gemma 2 27B
- GPT-4o
- Llama 3.3 70B
- Mistral 7B
- Phi-4 14B

The pipeline supported Gemini, Groq, local Ollama inference, and manual imports where an API was unavailable or too expensive. This let us compare a wide range of model sizes and reasoning behavior through the same order parser and simulator.

The event pool contained 120 test prompts, but the profitability averages only included outputs the parser could score. The valid count therefore differed by model. For example, the presentation reported 73 scored decisions for Gemini 1.5 Flash and 98 for Gemini 2.0 Flash. A malformed order was excluded rather than counted as a losing trade.

Gemini 1.5 Flash came closest to break-even at an average loss of `$85`, or `-0.17%`, per decision. Llama 3.3 70B followed at `-$144` and `-0.29%`. Gemini 2.0 Flash performed worst at `-$3,407` and `-6.81%`.

The models designed to reason longer did not reverse the result. Gemini 2.0 Thinking averaged `-$1,422` (`-2.84%`). The three DeepSeek R1 variants ranged from `-2.30%` to `-3.66%`. GPT-4o averaged `-3.74%`. None produced a positive average.

Netflix showed why a correct-looking narrative was not enough. The stock had already climbed sharply after hours and remained elevated before the open. GPT-4o still chose a `$200,000` long position with a five-percent target and a one-percent stop, then lost `$2,711` in our presentation's simulation. Other models also bought and lost money on the same event. They could summarize what had happened before 9:15 AM, but that did not mean they could predict what happened after 9:30 AM.

## What the benchmark did and did not show

We compared complete model configurations with different sizes, providers, and reasoning behavior. That made this a broad model benchmark. It did not isolate chain-of-thought length as a variable on its own.

The benchmark was also one short collection of earnings events. It showed how these configurations behaved in our evaluation window, not how they would behave in every market. Real deployment would add more problems: fees, slippage, changing liquidity, API latency, and the need to stop collecting market data early enough for slower models to finish before the opening bell.

<Callout variant="warning">
  This was a course benchmark and simulated evaluation, not a trading system or evidence that any model should manage real money.
</Callout>

What it did show clearly was that more reasoning text did not guarantee a useful action. The strongest-sounding explanation could end with a malformed order. A valid order could still choose a weaker direction according to our hindsight metric. Even the direction selected by that metric could be paired with a poor target, stop, or position size. Profitability depended on the whole chain.

## The part I carried forward

My checked-in contribution focused on the Jupyter analysis and visualizations. I built the return-versus-position scatter plot, model return distributions, exit-strategy comparison, and confusion matrices that compared each model's action with the pipeline's hindsight-selected action. Those charts helped us move past individual anecdotes and see that the losses were spread across the benchmark.

The project also gave me a concrete view of a data pipeline larger than one model call. There were API clients, time zones, symbol normalization, MongoDB aggregations, prompt generation, multiple inference providers, output parsing, simulation, and visualization. Each stage could change the final result.

I remember the project for that engineering work, and I also remember how I got there. A teammate took the time to help me learn JavaScript. I still use what she helped me start learning every day.

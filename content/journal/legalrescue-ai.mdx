<Side>← this number is the whole story</Side>

Two months in, the prototype was a Notion doc and a Loom. Six months later we
were running classification on real customer audio at <Hl>sub-2s P95 latency</Hl>.
This is what changed in between.

## The shape of the problem

<Side>people, not forms.</Side>

Walk-in clients calling a legal-aid line don't fill out intake forms — **they
tell their story**. Sometimes for forty minutes. The product turns that voice
memo into a structured case record a paralegal can act on in under two minutes.

<Figure src="/projects/stock-pred.png" alt="Review console">
The review console — a paralegal confirms the model's classification before it
commits to the case file. Humans stay in the loop where it counts.
</Figure>

The stack that got us there was deliberately boring around one non-boring model:

- `Whisper-large-v3` for speech-to-text, falling back to `v2` on cold-start
- `GPT-4.1-mini` for classification + summarization — the price/perf sweet spot
- FastAPI on AWS EKS, Celery + Redis for async workflows, Postgres for session state
- A custom prompt-eval harness to keep regressions out of prod

<Callout>
Budget: **2s P95**, end of audio to first structured field.[^1] Model choice,
queue depth and retry policy were all derived from that one number.
</Callout>

## Making async feel synchronous

<Side>the hardest part was never the LLM — it was the orchestration around it</Side>

Every request fans out into a queue the moment audio lands. The client holds a
lightweight job handle and polls; the UI streams partial state so the wait
<Hl>never reads as a dead spinner</Hl>.

```py title="intake.py" hi="3" cap="Hand back a handle immediately — never block the request."
@app.post("/intake")
async def intake(audio: UploadFile):
    job = await queue.enqueue(transcribe, audio)
    # client polls this handle; UI streams partial state
    return {"job_id": job.id, "status": "processing"}
```

<Quote cite="field notes, week 14">
The model is 5% of the work. The other 95% is everything around it.
</Quote>

## What I'd do differently

<Side>← learned this the hard way</Side>

Build the eval harness *first*. We bolted ours on three months in and immediately
wished we'd had it from week one — it would have caught two prompt regressions
that shipped to prod.

And pick a vector DB on day one, not day ninety. We bounced between three before
settling, and [each migration cost a sprint](#).

## Placeholder — the queue, in more detail

Placeholder paragraph. Every stage of the pipeline is a task, and every task is
idempotent, because the only thing worse than a job that fails is a job that
half-succeeds and then runs again. Retries carry the original request id, so a
duplicate submission collapses into the record that already exists rather than
creating a second case out of the same phone call.

Placeholder paragraph. The queue depth was tuned against the latency budget
rather than against throughput, which felt backwards at first. Optimising for
throughput produced a system that was faster on average and unpredictable at the
tail, and the tail is the part a paralegal actually experiences on a Tuesday
afternoon with somebody waiting.

## Placeholder — handling the audio nobody plans for

Placeholder paragraph. Real intake audio is not clean. It is a speakerphone in a
room with a television on, or a caller who puts the handset down for ninety
seconds, or forty minutes of narrative with the three load-bearing facts spread
evenly across it. Chunking on silence worked better than chunking on duration,
and a short overlap between chunks stopped the model from losing the thread at
the seams.

Placeholder paragraph. The summarisation prompt went through more revisions than
anything else in the system. Most of them were about what to leave out.

[^1]: P95, not P50. The long tail is where trust in the product actually lives.
